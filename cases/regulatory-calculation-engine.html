Designing a regulatory calculation engine

Translating complex regulatory rules into a maintainable and auditable information system.

Overview

Many regulated industries depend on calculations that must be applied consistently, transparently, and in accordance with evolving legal and technical frameworks.

This case study describes the design of a regulatory calculation engine that transformed a complex administrative calculation process into a structured and maintainable information system.

The objective was not simply to automate a calculation. The objective was to model regulatory knowledge in a way that could be implemented consistently, validated reliably, and evolved over time.

The challenge

The calculation process was governed by a regulatory framework containing multiple variables, conditional rules, technical parameters, and exceptional scenarios.

The primary challenges included:

  • interpreting regulatory requirements,
  • identifying calculation variables,
  • defining relationships between variables,
  • handling exceptional scenarios,
  • ensuring consistency across operational contexts,
  • supporting future regulatory changes.

The greatest complexity was not computational.

It was conceptual.

The regulation described the calculation in legal and technical language, while the software required explicit and structured business rules.

My role

My responsibility was to design the functional logic that would later be implemented in the information system.

This involved:

  • analyzing the regulatory framework,
  • identifying calculation variables,
  • modeling business rules,
  • defining decision logic,
  • validating calculation scenarios,
  • collaborating with technical teams during implementation,
  • supporting the evolution of the model over multiple years.

The calculation engine was initially represented through database-level business logic and later evolved toward a dedicated functional module.

The design approach

The first step was to separate regulatory knowledge from implementation technology.

Instead of focusing on SQL, the analysis focused on understanding:

  • what the regulation required,
  • how users applied the rules,
  • which decisions were deterministic,
  • which scenarios required exceptions,
  • how the calculation should be validated.

The calculation was decomposed into explicit business rules. Each rule was modeled independently before being integrated into the overall calculation process.

This significantly reduced ambiguity during implementation.

Design model

The calculation engine was designed by separating regulatory knowledge from implementation logic. The following model summarizes the transformation process from regulation to software.

Regulatory framework Legal and technical requirements
→
Business rules Variables, conditions, and decision logic
→
Information model Entities, relationships, and state transitions
→
Validation Scenarios, prototypes, and rule verification
→
Implementation SQL, application modules, and system integration

Figure 1. Knowledge-driven design flow used to translate regulatory requirements into an implementable information system.

Information model

The calculation engine required a structured representation of:

  • regulatory parameters,
  • operational variables,
  • user classifications,
  • technical conditions,
  • temporal conditions,
  • calculation outcomes,
  • validation states.

The objective was to create a model where regulatory logic could be traced and audited.

Design decisions

Explicit business rules

Regulatory logic was documented as business rules rather than implicit implementation behavior.

Separation of variables

Calculation variables were identified independently of database implementation.

Validation-first approach

Representative calculation scenarios were validated before implementation.

Evolution-oriented design

The model was designed with the expectation that regulations would change, reducing the long-term cost of maintaining the calculation engine.

The result

The resulting system provided:

  • consistent application of regulatory calculations,
  • reduced manual interpretation,
  • improved traceability,
  • easier validation,
  • greater maintainability,
  • a foundation for future functional evolution.

More importantly, the project demonstrated that the central problem was not software development.

It was knowledge modeling.

Design principle

Complex regulatory calculations should be modeled as business rule systems rather than embedded directly into implementation logic.

When regulatory knowledge is modeled explicitly, software becomes easier to validate, maintain, and evolve.

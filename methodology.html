Knowledge-Driven Systems Design

A practical approach for transforming business knowledge into information systems.

Most software projects begin with technology decisions. I begin with knowledge.

Over the years I have found that the greatest source of failure in complex software projects is not the programming language, the framework, or the infrastructure. It is the gap between how a domain works and how that domain is represented in a system.

My approach focuses on reducing that gap.

The objective is to transform business knowledge, regulatory frameworks, operational processes, and decision rules into a structured model that can be implemented consistently, validated systematically, and evolved over time.

This approach has been shaped by my experience designing information systems for complex regulatory and operational environments, where the quality of the specification often determines the quality of the implementation.

The design process

1. Understand the domain

The first objective is not to design a system. It is to understand the problem.

This includes business objectives, regulatory context, operational constraints, stakeholders, decision points, exceptions, and information flows.

The goal is to identify the knowledge that the organization relies on to perform the process.

2. Model the information

Once the domain is understood, the next step is to structure the information.

This involves identifying business entities, relationships, attributes, lifecycle states, transitions, and dependencies.

The information model becomes the foundation of the system.

3. Define business rules

Business rules should be explicit.

Instead of embedding knowledge directly into implementation, the objective is to identify calculation rules, validation rules, decision criteria, regulatory conditions, exception handling, and process constraints.

A well-defined business rule model reduces ambiguity during implementation.

4. Design the functional behavior

The functional design describes how the system should behave.

This includes workflows, user interactions, state transitions, approvals, integrations, notifications, and reporting requirements.

At this stage, the system is defined independently of implementation technology.

5. Validate the specification

Before implementation begins, the specification must be tested conceptually.

I often use functional prototypes, workflow simulations, rule validation, scenario analysis, and stakeholder reviews.

The objective is to discover ambiguity before software development.

6. Implement with technology

Only after the knowledge model is sufficiently clear does implementation become efficient.

Depending on the problem, implementation may involve SQL, Python, APIs, RAG architectures, Text-to-SQL, workflow automation, and AI-assisted development.

Technology becomes the implementation layer of a previously validated knowledge model.

7. Evaluate and evolve

A system is not a static artifact.

The final stage focuses on information quality, user feedback, process evolution, regulatory changes, performance indicators, and continuous refinement.

The model should be designed to evolve without requiring complete redesign.

Why AI matters

Artificial intelligence is a powerful accelerator, but it does not replace domain understanding.

In my work, AI is used to:

  • accelerate information analysis,
  • generate prototypes,
  • assist specification development,
  • improve knowledge retrieval,
  • support testing,
  • reduce repetitive implementation work.

The quality of AI-assisted development depends heavily on the quality of the underlying specification.

A simple principle

Business logic is independent of implementation technology.

A calculation remains the same whether it is implemented in SQL, Python, Java, or another language.

The real challenge is understanding the rule.

That is why I focus on modeling knowledge first and implementing technology second.

The role of case studies

The case studies in this portfolio are examples of this methodology applied to real-world problems.

They are presented in a generalized form to respect confidentiality, but each one reflects the same underlying process:

understand → model → specify → validate → implement → evolve
